AI Is Not the Hero

You are. The technology is a supporting character, and it does not get to enter until Act Three.

I sat with an insurance agent whose people were spending eight hours a day on the phone. Not selling — collecting names, dates of birth, addresses, and chasing old leads.

Six of them. Eight hours apiece. Forty-eight hours a day of licensed professionals doing intake.

Everybody's first instinct is the same: put a bot on it. That is the wrong first move. Before I put one piece of technology anywhere near that business, I asked him what his business model actually was.

The question is never "what can we automate." It is: what should stop requiring a human — and what should only ever be a human?

They did not need to be automated. They needed to be humanized. The intake never needed a licensed anybody. The conversation where somebody is deciding how to protect their family? That should never be anything but him.

If you automate confusion, you just get confusion faster.

Bleeding · Feeding · Needing

The recurring engine. Three questions, asked in this order, before a single tool is chosen.

BLEEDING

Where are time, money, energy, leads, or opportunities leaking?

At your level the bleed is not sloppiness — it is rate. It is a four-hundred-dollar hour spent on twenty-two-dollar work. And most owners never call it a process problem. They call it being busy.

FEEDING

What is actually feeding revenue — and what should get more attention?

Name the one thing that, when it is moving, the money is moving. Every hour spent elsewhere is an hour it isn't. Don't starve what's working because you're busy fixing everything else.

NEEDING

What does the business need next to hit the goal?

Not forever. Next. Then pick one thing that should stop depending on you and put a date on it — because a decision without a date is a wish.

Twenty-Five Years, No Contract

He never once told me he had a process problem. He told me he was busy.

A man called me. Twenty-five years driving celebrities. Good money, real business. And he was telling me how it ran — while he was driving. Taking a client call at seventy miles an hour, pen and paper on the passenger seat.

Before

  • No contract. Twenty-five years, and there was no contract.
  • Collecting on Zelle — and driving clients to an ATM when they didn't have it.
  • Drove across town for pickups that evaporated. He ate the gas and the time.
  • Intake by pen and paper, on the seat, while driving.
  • No service answering the phone. If he didn't pick up, he didn't get the business.

After

  • A receptionist that never sleeps. Someone books a limo at 11pm — they get booked.
  • Contract by email. Payment link by email. They pay before he turns a key.
  • A no-show now costs the customer instead of costing him.
  • The phone gets answered while he is driving — the only time it ever rang.
  • Two dashboards: what he's making today, what he made yesterday.

He resigned from the job of dispatcher. And he went from a man taking whatever ride came through the phone that hour — to a man with a calendar. Now he books weddings.

The only hours he could earn were the only hours he could not sell.
He came in as the operator of it. He walked out as the architect of it.
I could hand you five tools right now. And you would end up with five new jobs — watching agents.

That is not leverage. That is a second shift. When there is confusion, nothing gets done — which is exactly why the technology comes last, not first.
