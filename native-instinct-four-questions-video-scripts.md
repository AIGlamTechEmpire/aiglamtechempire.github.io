# Native Instinct: Four Questions Video Scripts (Pinky's voice, business-professional audience)

Pinky's warmth and dry humor, tuned for owners, executives and operations leaders: no pet names, less Southern colloquialism, more business language. Each block is a spoken transcript only (about 60 seconds), so everything inside is read aloud. There are no labels or stage directions inside the blocks.

Topic source: the "Where we start" section of the Native Instinct page.

Each Short has a light laugh, one concrete detail, and one coined line a viewer can carry out:
1. "A leak with good manners."
2. "A job with a business card."
3. "Remembering is not a system. It is a hope."
4. "A plan in a binder is a decoration."

Note: the dollar figures in Short 1 are illustrative arithmetic on made-up inputs. No personal anecdotes or facts about Pinky were invented. If she wants a real story in any Short, add one in place of the example.

---

```
Short 1

Let me show you how a twenty-minute task costs a business almost nine thousand dollars a year. Say someone on your team writes up client intake notes. Twenty minutes, five times a week. That is nearly two hours every week, and about eighty-seven hours over a year. Now value that time at a hundred dollars an hour. You are looking at roughly eighty-seven hundred dollars, and it never appeared as a line on a single report. I call that a leak with good manners. It does not flood the office. It just drips quietly while everyone walks past it with a coffee. This is why we price the work before we touch any technology. What gets repeated, how often, how long it takes, and what that time is worth. You cannot fix a leak you have never measured, and most leadership teams have never seen theirs in dollars.
```

```
Short 2

Here is a simple stress test for your business. Imagine you are unreachable for three days. No phone, no email. What stops? Invoices waiting on your approval. A team stalled because only you know the answer. A client message sitting unanswered because the process lives in your head. If the honest answer is everything, then you do not have systems. You have one person doing six jobs, and that person is the owner. That is not a failure. It is how most businesses get built. But a business that cannot run without you is a job with a business card. The fix is practical. Take the three tasks that consume the most of your time and document each one. The tools, the roles, the steps in order, and the point where quality gets checked. Once it is written down, someone else can run it, and three days away is just three days.
```

```
Short 3

Most leaders can tell you where their revenue comes from. Referrals, repeat clients, the website, the sales team. That is the first half of the question. The second half is whether it arrives consistently, and that is where businesses get surprised. Plenty of companies can name every source and still ride a strong quarter straight into a quiet one. When that happens, the sources are rarely the problem. The handoffs are. A lead comes in and no one follows up. A project closes and no one asks for the referral. Each of those moments depends on someone remembering, and remembering is not a system. It is a hope. So the work has two parts. Map every source from first contact to booked revenue. Then protect each handoff so it happens without you chasing it. Consistent revenue is not luck. It is a path that someone mapped and then guarded.
```

```
Short 4

You do not need a five-year plan to close a revenue gap. You need a thirty-day one, and it has three parts. Start with the goal and where you are today. The distance between them is the gap. Now ask what has to change first. Part one is the priority: the single place where time or money is leaking the most. One, not five. Part two is the first process: the one task you document so it stops living in a single person's head. Part three is the sequence for the next thirty days, what happens first, second and third, starting this week. A plan in a binder is a decoration. Big plans stall because they are too big to start. A small first move, aimed at the largest leak, is how a gap actually begins to close. Start there.
```
